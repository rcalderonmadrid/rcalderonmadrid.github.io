Postdoctoral project at the University of Salford combining airborne hyperspectral and thermal imagery, machine learning and epidemic spread models to detect Xylella fastidiosa before symptoms appear and to improve surveillance in olive (Italy) and almond (Spain).

Overview

Background

Xylella fastidiosa is considered one of the most dangerous plant pathogenic bacteria in the world. First detected in Europe in 2013 in olive trees in Apulia (southern Italy), where it causes Olive Quick Decline Syndrome, it has since been found in France, Spain and other countries, and it can infect more than 550 host species. Its spread threatens the large Mediterranean areas planted with olive, almond, citrus and grapevine, and the EU has made early detection a cornerstone of its plant health regulation.

Epidemiological models are increasingly used to simulate how epidemics spread across landscapes and to design surveillance and control strategies that maximise the chances of eradication while minimising costs. Remote sensing, in turn, can monitor very large areas quickly and, with high-resolution hyperspectral and thermal sensors, detect the physiological changes caused by vascular pathogens.

Knowledge gaps

  • Long asymptomatic period. Olive trees infected by X. fastidiosa can take around 10 months or more to show symptoms, while surveillance in Europe relies mainly on visual inspection. Infected but asymptomatic trees keep spreading the pathogen undetected.
  • Costly and biased field surveillance. Field surveys are labour-intensive and expensive, so they are limited to small areas and are often spatially biased, giving a distorted picture of the real state of the epidemic.
  • Poorly parameterised models. Spread models depend on parameters estimated from scarce and imperfect inspection data, which limits their ability to predict emerging epidemics accurately.
  • Remote sensing and spread models were disconnected. It was unclear what remote sensing could reveal about epidemic dynamics, and how it could be combined with spread models to estimate disease distribution and to target surveillance.

Project aim: to optimise the surveillance and early detection of Xylella fastidiosa in the European Union by integrating epidemic spread models with airborne hyperspectral and thermal remote sensing, from the detection of pre-visual infections to the design of surveillance and control strategies.

Study areas

  • Apulia, Italy: X. fastidiosa subsp. pauca in olive orchards, with airborne campaigns in 2016 and 2017 and field inspections of about 3,300 trees per year in 15 plots.
  • Alicante, Spain: X. fastidiosa subsp. multiplex in almond orchards, with an airborne campaign in 2018 and field inspections of 1,453 trees in 20 plots, 328 of them tested by qPCR.

Airborne campaigns were carried out by the European Commission Joint Research Centre and IAS-CSIC within the H2020 projects XF-ACTORS and POnTE.

Key results at a glance

80.9% accuracy detecting X. fastidiosa in olive from plant traits

61% of asymptomatic trees flagged by remote sensing showed symptoms within 4 months (vs 39%)

35% → 50% qPCR-confirmed infections detected in almond after coupling the spread model

Approach: three work packages

1 · Remote sensing detection

Automatic methods for the early detection of X. fastidiosa at landscape scale from airborne hyperspectral and thermal imagery:

  • Plant functional traits retrieved by radiative transfer model inversion (pigments, structure, nitrogen-related indices), chlorophyll fluorescence and canopy temperature.
  • Machine learning classifiers (support vector machines, neural networks, linear discriminant analysis) trained on field inspections.
  • Multi-temporal revisits of trees over two years, supported by qPCR, to test pre-visual detection.

2 · Estimating disease distribution

A stochastic optimisation model that estimates the spatial distribution of X. fastidiosa from a sample by combining:

  • Landscape connectivity of the host population (graph and circuit theory) and distance-dependent dispersal.
  • Remote sensing infection probabilities for each tree.

Developed and validated in Italy and then transferred to the almond orchards of Alicante.

3 · Spatio-temporal spread modelling

A stochastic, spatially explicit spread model for X. fastidiosa in Italy, parameterised with two data sources, field inspections and remote sensing, to compare:

  • The epidemiological parameters estimated from each source.
  • The effectiveness of different surveillance and control scenarios, such as revisit intervals, detection probability and host removal radius.

Main findings

Pre-visual detection

  • Plant traits related to pigments and structure, together with chlorophyll fluorescence and temperature, detected infected olive trees with 80.9% accuracy (κ = 0.61), against 65.4% using only the RGB–NIR indices typical of satellite sensors.
  • Trees flagged by remote sensing but still asymptomatic in the field were significantly more likely to develop symptoms in the following months, confirming that infection can be detected before visible symptoms appear.

Almond orchards in Alicante

  • Adding nitrogen-related indices from the SWIR region to fluorescence and temperature gave the best detection of X. fastidiosa in almond (74.0% accuracy, κ = 0.48).
  • Accuracy dropped in orchards with low disease incidence, precisely where early detection matters most.

Remote sensing + spread models

  • Combining remote sensing with landscape connectivity and dispersal gave more realistic estimates of disease distribution in low-incidence orchards than remote sensing alone.
  • Coupling the spread model with remote sensing increased the detection of qPCR-confirmed infections in almond from 35% to 50%.

Take-home message

High-resolution hyperspectral and thermal remote sensing can detect Xylella fastidiosa infections before symptoms are visible, and combining it with epidemic spread models improves estimates of where the disease is, especially at the early stages of an outbreak when control is still possible. This work opened a line of research that I continue today in the MOMENTUM project, moving these approaches to satellite data and operational surveillance.
